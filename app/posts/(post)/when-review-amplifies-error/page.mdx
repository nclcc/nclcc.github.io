# When review amplifies error

Adding another AI reviewer does not automatically make an answer safer. A reviewer can correct
mistakes, but it can also propagate an existing mistake into dependent claims, calculations, or
recommendations. The relevant question is not simply whether a model is accurate in isolation. It
is whether the review process reduces the cost of unresolved mistakes over time.

[Download the white paper (PDF)](/papers/error-dynamics-in-bot-conversations.pdf)

## A cost process, not just a correctness score

Let `Eₜ` be the cost of the mistakes that remain after review round `t`. Cost can combine
financial impact, safety exposure, remediation effort, and downstream reliance. Let `cₜ` be the
fraction of existing cost corrected in the next review, and let `rₜ` be the downstream cost
induced per unit of residual error. Then:

```
Eₜ₊₁ = (1 − cₜ + rₜ)Eₜ
```

The model is deliberately simple. Correction reduces the existing stock; amplification creates new
cost because an unresolved error influences what follows.

## The threshold is clear

Over `T` rounds,

```
E_T = E₀ ∏ₜ₌₀ᵀ⁻¹ Gₜ
```

For constant rates, error cost grows when `r > c`: a review chain propagates more cost than it
removes. With variable rates, the relevant typical-path measure is the long-run log-growth rate,
`γ = E[log Gₜ]`, under the usual stationarity and ergodicity conditions.

- `γ < 0`: error cost contracts.
- `γ = 0`: the process is critical.
- `γ > 0`: error cost grows exponentially.

This does not claim that every multi-agent workflow is unstable. It gives a criterion that can be
measured, challenged, and compared across review protocols.

## Why model diversity is not enough

Different agents can still share sources, training data, prompts, and blind spots. In a multivariate
version of the model, error costs are tracked across categories and a propagation matrix captures
how an error in one category creates cost in another. The dominant eigenvalue of that matrix
determines whether the coupled system contracts or grows in the long run.

The practical implication is straightforward: benchmark accuracy is not a reliability guarantee for
a conversation chain. Teams should measure correction, induced error cost, dependence between
reviewers, and tail risk from rare but expensive mistakes.

The paper develops the full framework, including stochastic and multivariate variants, conditions
for explosive growth, and an empirical protocol for testing the model on tasks with verifiable
ground truth.
